## Why is "the faster loop wins" the wrong lesson?

The OODA loop, the observe, orient, decide and act cycle described by the US Air Force strategist John Boyd, is often quoted in business as a simple rule: whoever cycles faster wins. Leaders who apply that rule to AI tend to invest in the first step. Better models observe more, sooner.

It is an understandable reading, and it misses the larger point of Boyd's work. His briefings, collected in [A Discourse on Winning and Losing](https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/Portals/10/AUPress/Books/B_0151_Boyd_Discourse_Winning_Losing.pdf) by Air University Press, place orientation, the shared understanding that shapes what people notice and how they respond, at the centre of the loop. Speed follows from good orientation; it does not replace it.

For organisations adopting AI, that distinction matters. AI has made observing fast and cheap. In my judgement, most of the delay between a signal and a response now sits in orienting and deciding: agreeing what the signal means, and who may act on it.

## What does AI change in the loop, and what does it not change?

AI changes the first two stages most. It observes continuously, at a scale no team could match, and it helps orient by grouping evidence and suggesting explanations.

AI does not change the last two stages on its own. Deciding requires someone with authority and an agreed view of acceptable risk. Acting requires access to the levers that change what happens next. If those are missing, a faster observation stage produces a longer queue of signals waiting for a decision.

## Find the value: where does your loop actually stall?

The first step is to look at one important signal and time each stage of the loop for recent events. When did the signal fire? When did someone understand what it meant? When was a decision made? When did the action take effect?

The answer is often surprising. The model may flag a problem within minutes, while understanding takes an hour of messages and the decision waits for a meeting the next day. Where the time goes tells leaders where investment will pay. A better model helps only if observing is the slow stage.

**Decision for the sponsor:** for the signals that matter most, which stage of the loop takes longest, and what is that delay costing?

## Lead the change: how do leaders shorten orienting and deciding?

Boyd's answer to slow orientation and decision was not more central control. It was shared understanding and clear intent, so that people close to events could act without waiting for orders. Military doctrine calls the second idea commander's intent: a short statement of the outcome wanted and the limits within which people may act.

In a business adopting AI, intent might read: protect customers' funds first, accept some friction for new accounts during an attack, and escalate anything that affects more than a set number of customers. With that intent written down, the person who sees the signal can orient quickly, because they know what matters, and can decide within limits, because they know what they may do.

Three leadership choices make this practical:

- **Shared orientation.** Agree in advance what each important signal means and what it usually requires, so that understanding does not have to be built from scratch each time.
- **Written intent.** State the outcome to protect and the cost the organisation will accept to protect it.
- **Bounded authority.** Name the role that may act, and how far, as set out in [who should be allowed to act on an AI signal](/2026-08-20-assign-decision-rights-now-how-to-turn-ai-signals-into-fast-authorized-actions/).

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a payments team receives an alert that card-testing attempts have tripled on one checkout page. Without written intent, the analyst on duty asks a manager, who asks the product owner whether friction is acceptable. With intent already agreed, the analyst knows that protecting customers' cards comes before conversion on that page, adds verification for the affected traffic, and records why.

**Decision for the sponsor:** what is our intent for each critical signal, and who has the authority to act on it without waiting?

## Prove the impact: is the loop shorter, and are the decisions better?

A shorter loop is valuable only if the decisions remain sound. Measure both. For each important signal, track the time spent in each stage before and after the change. Alongside speed, track quality: losses contained, actions later reversed, and customer friction caused.

Review a sample of decisions regularly against the stated intent. Were people acting within it? Did the intent itself prove wrong in some situations? Boyd's loop applies to the organisation as well as to events: leaders observe how their own decision process performs, and adjust it.

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, is the loop faster without becoming careless, and should the intent or authority be adjusted?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The main cost is leadership time: agreeing intent, defining what signals mean, and delegating authority, often against the instinct to keep decisions central. Training and practice take time too, because orientation is built by working through real cases together.

The simpler alternative is to start with one signal: time its loop, write its intent in a paragraph, and name the role that may act. That costs little and shows whether the bottleneck is really where the organisation assumed. Where the slow stage turns out to be observing, investing in detection may then be the right call, as discussed in [should you invest in AI for supply chain loss prevention](/2026-11-23-should-you-invest-in-ai-for-supply-chain-loss-prevention-buy-the-decision-not-the-alerts/).

## Questions to put to your team

1. For our most important signal, how long does each stage of the loop take?
2. Do the people who see the signal know what outcome they are protecting?
3. Who may act, and within what limits, without waiting for a meeting?
4. Is our investment going to the stage of the loop that is actually slow?
5. How will we know that faster decisions are still good decisions?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the OODA loop?

The OODA loop is a decision cycle described by the US Air Force strategist John Boyd: observe, orient, decide, act. Observing gathers information, orienting makes sense of it, deciding chooses a response and acting carries it out. Boyd argued that how well and how quickly an organisation moves through this cycle shapes its results.

### How does AI change the OODA loop?

AI mainly speeds up observing, and partly orienting, by detecting patterns earlier and summarising evidence. It does not decide who may act or what risk is acceptable. If those questions are unanswered, faster observation simply means problems are seen sooner and still handled at the speed of the slowest approval.

### What is commander's intent in a business setting?

It is a short statement of the outcome a leader wants and the limits within which people may act to achieve it. For an AI signal, it might say what must be protected, what cost is acceptable and which actions need approval. It lets people act sensibly in situations nobody anticipated in detail.
