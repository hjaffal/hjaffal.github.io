## Why can a healthy-looking metric be dangerous?

Every operation has measures that are reliably green. They appear at the top of the report, they rarely move and they reassure everyone who sees them. Some of them deserve that trust. Others describe something easier than the thing leaders care about, and they reassure precisely when they should be prompting questions.

Leaders usually treat a stable, green measure as evidence that all is well. It is a natural reading, and it misses a larger question: is this measure capable of telling us when something is wrong? A measure that cannot turn red is not a control; it is decoration.

In my judgement, false confidence is more dangerous than an obviously broken report, because a broken report invites scrutiny and a reassuring one discourages it.

## What does AI change about misleading metrics, and what does it not change?

AI changes how many measures an organisation can produce and how polished they look. Dashboards, summaries and commentary can now be generated quickly and at scale.

AI does not change whether a measure answers the right question. A model can make a misleading measure look more authoritative, and it can be trained on targets that themselves give false confidence. The judgement about what a measure really shows remains with leaders.

## Find the value: which of our measures give false confidence?

Five tests identify most false-confidence measures:

- **Activity rather than outcome.** It counts work done, such as tickets closed or calls made, rather than the result that work should achieve, such as issues resolved for good.
- **Variation hidden in an average.** A healthy overall figure conceals a serious problem in one segment, site or product.
- **No decision in months.** Nobody can name a decision the measure has changed in the last quarter.
- **It never turns red.** The target is set where the measure always meets it, so it cannot warn.
- **Easy to move without improving anything.** The people measured by it can change the number without changing the result, for example by closing tickets early.

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a support team reports a high satisfaction score every month. The score comes only from customers whose issues were closed, so customers who gave up and left are never counted. Satisfaction stays high while churn in one product line rises.

**Decision for the sponsor:** which of our most-viewed measures fail two or more of these tests?

## Lead the change: how do leaders turn reassurance into warning?

The fix is rarely to delete the measure. It is to change what it can tell you:

- **Pair it with an outcome.** Report tickets closed alongside issues reopened; calls made alongside customers retained.
- **Show the variation.** Report the spread and the worst segments, not only the average.
- **Give it a threshold that triggers action.** Define the level at which someone must act, who that is and what they do, so the measure can turn red and mean something when it does.
- **Make it harder to game.** Choose measures that are difficult to move without improving the result, a problem examined in [how to stop teams gaming the metrics they are judged on](/2026-06-08-your-metrics-are-being-gamed-most-bi-teams-and-analysts-operate-under-a-delusion/).

Ownership matters here too. Each false-confidence measure needs someone accountable for the paired outcome, not only for the original number. Otherwise the team that produces tickets closed is never asked about tickets reopened, and the measure goes on reassuring the people who read it while the result it was meant to describe slips.

**Decision for the sponsor:** for each false-confidence measure, what outcome will we pair it with, and what threshold will trigger action?

## Prove the impact: do our measures now warn us in time?

The test of the change is whether measures now warn before problems become large. Look back at recent problems and ask whether any measure turned red early enough to act. Where none did, a false-confidence measure may still be standing in for a real one.

Track how often each measure triggers action and how often that action proves worthwhile. A measure that never triggers may still be giving false confidence; one that triggers constantly may need a better threshold. The wider discipline of keeping only measures that change decisions is set out in [which three questions decide whether a metric is worth keeping](/2026-11-12-if-a-metric-wont-change-your-next-move-cut-it-three-questions-that-make-numbers-intelligent/).

Expect some measures to look worse once they are paired with outcomes and shown with their variation. That is not deterioration; it is the measure starting to tell the truth. Leaders who respond to the first red numbers with curiosity rather than blame will find that red numbers start to appear earlier.

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, are our measures now warning us in time, and which still only reassure?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The costs are modest in data and larger in comfort. Pairing measures with outcomes and showing variation makes reports less reassuring, and some teams will see measures they were proud of turn amber or red. That discomfort is the point.

The simpler first step is to apply the five tests to the five measures leaders look at most. It takes an hour and usually finds at least one measure that has been reassuring the organisation without informing it.

It also helps to ask, for each measure, who would be the first to know if the reality it describes got worse. If the answer is a customer, a regulator or a newspaper rather than the person who reads the measure, it is giving false confidence and should be redesigned.

## Questions to put to your team

1. Which of our most-viewed measures count activity rather than results?
2. Which ones hide a serious problem inside a healthy average?
3. When did each of them last change a decision?
4. Has each of them turned red in the last year?
5. Could the people measured by them move the number without improving anything?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a false-confidence metric?

It is a measure that presents a positive or stable picture while failing to capture the risk or result it is supposed to describe. It is not usually wrong in itself. It answers an easier question than the one leaders think it answers, and so it reassures them when they should be asking more.

### What is the difference between an activity metric and an outcome metric?

An activity metric counts work done, such as tickets closed, calls made or reports produced. An outcome metric measures the result that work is meant to achieve, such as issues resolved for good, customers retained or losses reduced. Activity can rise while outcomes fall, which is why activity metrics so often create false confidence.

### Should false-confidence metrics be deleted?

Not always. Some are useful to the team doing the work, as long as they are not mistaken for evidence of results. The usual fix is to pair them with an outcome measure, report the variation behind the average, and attach a threshold that triggers action, so the measure can warn rather than only reassure.
