## Why do the strongest analysts end up producing reports?

In many organisations, the analysts with the most context are given the most reports. They understand the business best, so they are asked to explain last week's numbers, prepare the slides for the leadership meeting and answer the follow-up questions. Their week fills with describing the past, while the signals that will shape next month go unexamined.

Leaders usually ask analysts for faster, clearer reporting. It is a reasonable request, and it misses a larger question: how much of the organisation's most valuable analytical time should go to explaining what happened, rather than to changing what happens next? Reporting explains the past. Analysis that leads to action changes the future.

In my judgement, the balance in most organisations tilts heavily towards reporting, not because anyone chose it, but because recurring requests accumulate and are rarely retired.

## What does AI change in analysts' work, and what does it not change?

AI changes the cost of producing reports. Summaries, standard charts and routine commentary can now be generated quickly, which should free analyst time for harder questions.

AI does not change what leaders ask for or reward. If the organisation continues to value polished decks delivered on time, the freed time will be filled with more decks. The shift from reporting to analysis is a leadership choice, not a technical one.

## Find the value: how is analyst time actually spent?

The first step is to look. For a month, record how the team's time divides between recurring reporting, ad hoc explanation of past results, and forward-looking work: finding signals, proposing actions and checking results. Then list the recurring reports and ask, for each, what decision it supported in the last quarter.

The answers usually show two things. Some reports support no decision anyone can name, and can be stopped. Others support real decisions but could be replaced by something shorter. Together they show how much analytical capacity is available to redirect.

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a fraud analytics team finds that its most experienced analyst spends three days a week preparing the weekly risk deck. Replacing the deck with a one-page decision memo frees most of that time, which goes instead to watching the signals that predict next month's losses.

**Decision for the sponsor:** how much of our analysts' time goes to reporting, and which reports supported a decision last quarter?

## Lead the change: how do leaders shift analyst time towards action?

Four changes make the shift practical:

- **Retire reports nobody acts on.** Pause them, with a simple route for anyone who genuinely needs one back. The approach is described in [how to retire metrics without losing control](/2026-06-18-kill-metrics-that-dont-change-decisions-a-6-step-playbook-to-replace-reporting-with-intelligence/).
- **Replace decks with decision memos.** A one-page memo states the signal, the recommended action, its owner, the cost of acting and of not acting, and how the result will be checked.
- **Connect analysts to the people who act.** Analysts working on a risk or opportunity should have a direct line to the role that can change the control, price or process, rather than reporting upwards and waiting.
- **Change what is rewarded.** Recognise analysts for recommendations acted on and results achieved, not for slide quality or report punctuality.

There is a trade-off. Analysts closer to action will sometimes recommend changes that prove unnecessary, and some leaders will miss their familiar reports. Both costs are usually smaller than the value of analysis that arrives in time to matter. The question of who may act on what analysts find is covered in [who should be allowed to act on an AI signal](/2026-08-20-assign-decision-rights-now-how-to-turn-ai-signals-into-fast-authorized-actions/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** which reports will we retire, and what will we ask analysts for instead?

## Prove the impact: is analyst time now changing decisions?

Measure what analysts' work changes. Track the number of recommendations acted on, the time from an analyst identifying a signal to a change being made, and the results of those changes against a baseline. Track the share of time spent on reporting too, to see whether the balance has actually shifted.

Review a sample of decision memos after their results are known. Were the recommendations sound? Were they acted on quickly? Did the actions work? That review improves both the analysis and the decisions.

Expect the balance to shift gradually. Some reporting will always be needed, for regulation, audit or planning. The aim is not to end reporting but to make sure that what remains is justified by a decision, and that the strongest analysts spend most of their time where they add most.

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, is analysts' time producing more decisions and better results, and how far should the shift go?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The costs are mostly in habit and expectation. Leaders who rely on familiar reports must accept shorter memos; analysts must accept accountability for recommendations, not just accuracy; and some reports that felt important will be missed for a while.

The simpler first step is to take one recurring report, replace it for a month with a one-page decision memo, and compare what each produced. Turning a single weekly report into something that triggers action is described in [how to turn a weekly report into something that triggers action](/2026-07-23-how-to-convert-a-weekly-report-into-an-intelligence-product-that-triggers-action/).

Leaders should also protect the time once it is freed. Without a deliberate choice about what analysts will work on instead, new report requests tend to fill the space within weeks. Naming the forward-looking questions each analyst owns is the simplest way to keep the balance from drifting back.

## Questions to put to your team

1. What share of our analysts' time goes to recurring reporting?
2. Which of our reports supported a decision in the last quarter?
3. Do our analysts have a direct line to the people who can act on what they find?
4. Do we reward recommendations acted on, or reports delivered on time?
5. What would our strongest analyst work on if half their reporting disappeared?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between reporting and analysis?

Reporting describes what happened, in a consistent format, usually on a schedule. Analysis asks why it happened and what should be done, and ends with a recommendation. Both are useful. The problem arises when reporting consumes the time of the people best placed to do analysis, and recommendations stop being made.

### What is a decision memo?

A decision memo is a one-page document that replaces a long report or deck. It states the signal, what it means, the action recommended, who owns the action, the cost of acting and of not acting, and how the result will be checked. It is written to be decided on, not just read.

### How do you measure whether analysts are creating value?

Track what their work changes, not how much they produce. Useful measures include the number of recommendations acted on, the time from an analyst identifying a signal to a change being made, and the results of those changes. Slide counts and report deadlines measure activity, not value.
