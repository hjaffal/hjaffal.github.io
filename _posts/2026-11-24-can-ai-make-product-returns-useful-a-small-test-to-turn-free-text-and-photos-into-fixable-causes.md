## Why are returns usually treated only as a cost?

Product returns are usually managed as a cost line: processing, transport, write-offs and fraud. The question executives bring is therefore how to make returns cheaper. It is a fair question, and it misses a larger one: what are customers telling the business about products it could fix?

The scale makes the question worth asking. US retail returns reached an estimated $890 billion in 2024, 16.9% of annual sales, according to the [National Retail Federation (NRF) and Happy Returns](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/nrf-and-happy-returns-report-2024-retail-returns-total-890-billion). Much of the information about why those products came back is already in the business, written by customers and agents, and rarely read at scale.

The question this article answers is narrower than "can AI read returns?" It can. The question is whether what it reads will change a decision, and whether that change is worth what it costs.

## What does AI change in returns analysis, and what does it not change?

AI changes the cost of reading. A model can now process a customer's free-text reason, an agent's notes and a photo, and assign a consistent cause, at a volume no team could read by hand. Causes that were recorded as "other" or "changed my mind" can be grouped into something a product owner recognises: unclear sizing, damage in transit, a missing part, a description that oversells.

AI does not decide what to fix, who pays for the fix or whether the fix worked. Better classification is valuable only if it changes a decision: a size chart, a packaging specification, a supplier conversation or a returns policy. If no one is ready to make those decisions, better data produces better reports and the same returns.

## Find the value: which returns are worth understanding?

The value of returns analysis sits in the fixable share. A fixable return is one caused by something the business controls, such as product information, packaging, quality or fulfilment, rather than by a customer's changing preference.

The value pool is that fixable share, multiplied by the volume of returns and by the full cost of each one: transport, handling, refurbishment or write-off, and the customer service time around it. In my judgement, the pool is rarely spread evenly. It tends to be concentrated in a small number of product lines, where one cause repeats.

Sizing this does not require AI. A sample of a few hundred recent returns from candidate product lines, read and categorised by experienced staff, gives a first estimate of the fixable share and shows where it concentrates. That estimate also becomes the reference set: a sample labelled by your own experts, against which any model is later judged.

**Decision for the sponsor:** is the fixable pool large enough to justify a structured test, and in which product lines is it concentrated?

## Lead the change: who must act on what the AI finds?

The people who need to act on returns analysis rarely own the returns process. A sizing cause is fixed by whoever owns product content, a packaging cause by operations or a supplier, and a quality cause by sourcing. If the analysis is commissioned by the returns team alone, its findings arrive in a report that no one with the power to change the product is obliged to read.

Leading this change means agreeing three things before any results arrive. First, the owner of each likely cause, by role. Second, the threshold that commits them to act: for example, if a given cause accounts for more than an agreed share of a product line's returns, the owner changes the product page or packaging within a set time. Third, the budget and time each owner may spend without further approval.

Agreeing thresholds in advance matters because results that arrive without them invite debate rather than action. A finding that "a fifth of returns in this line cite sizing" is interesting. A finding that crosses a threshold someone already accepted is a decision. The same principle applies to any AI signal, as discussed in [who should be allowed to act on an AI signal](/2026-08-20-assign-decision-rights-now-how-to-turn-ai-signals-into-fast-authorized-actions/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** who owns each likely fix, and what result commits them to act?

## Prove the impact: did fewer products come back?

Proving the impact means separating two questions that are often merged: did the AI classify returns well, and did acting on its classification reduce returns?

For the first question, measure agreement between the model and the reference set, and look closely at the disagreements that would change a decision. A model that confuses two kinds of damage may not matter. A model that confuses sizing with damage sends the fix to the wrong team.

For the second question, compare like with like. When a product line changes its size guide or packaging, compare its return rate for that cause before and after, against a similar line that did not change. Track the time from a return arriving to a usable cause, the share of findings that triggered an agreed action, and the cost per action.

Set a stop rule before the test begins: the result below which the organisation will stop or narrow the work. Stopping on evidence is a result, not a failure. The broader discipline of keeping only measures that change a decision is set out in [which three questions decide whether a metric is worth keeping](/2026-11-12-if-a-metric-wont-change-your-next-move-cut-it-three-questions-that-make-numbers-intelligent/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, should the organisation scale the analysis to more product lines, narrow it, or stop?

## What does it cost, and what is the cheaper alternative?

The model is rarely the main cost. The larger costs are joining returns data held in several systems, expert time to build the reference set, review of uncertain cases, and the changes to products, pages and packaging that the analysis recommends. Privacy needs attention too: photos and notes can contain personal details that the analysis does not need.

The cheaper alternative deserves a fair comparison. A clearer returns form with more specific reasons, and a required photo for damage claims, may produce enough signal to act on without AI. If a better form shows the fixable causes clearly, the right answer may be not to invest in AI at all, or to invest later, once the simpler change has been exhausted.

## Questions to put to your team

1. What share of our returns do we believe is fixable, and how do we know?
2. In which product lines is that share concentrated?
3. Who owns each likely fix, and have they agreed the threshold that commits them to act?
4. What would a better returns form tell us without AI?
5. What result would make us stop?

## Frequently asked questions

### Can AI tell why a product was returned?

Often, yes. Current models can read a customer's free-text reason, an agent's notes and an attached photo, and assign a consistent cause such as sizing, damage in transit or a missing part. Accuracy should be measured against a sample your own experts have labelled, not assumed from a vendor demonstration.

### How long should a returns analysis test run?

Long enough to see an action and its effect, which usually means several weeks rather than days. The analysis itself can be ready quickly. The time goes into agreeing action thresholds, making the change, such as a new size guide, and then comparing return rates against a product line that did not change.

### What does AI returns analysis cost?

The model is rarely the largest cost. Expect to spend more on joining returns data from several systems, expert time to label a reference sample, reviewing uncertain cases, and the product or packaging changes themselves. Count all of these before comparing the investment with simpler alternatives.

### Can AI separate return fraud from genuine returns?

It can flag patterns worth reviewing, but fraud is a different decision with different owners and a higher cost of error. Keep fraud detection separate from cause analysis, so that the goal of fixing products is not confused with the goal of refusing customers.
