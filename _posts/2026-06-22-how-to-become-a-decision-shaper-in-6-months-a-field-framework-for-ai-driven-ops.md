## Why does the move from operating tools to shaping decisions feel so hard?

Many people who work with data and AI tools know that the valuable work is moving towards decisions. What is less clear is how to get there. Shaping decisions sounds like something that comes with a senior title, rather than something you can build towards from your current role.

The usual question is how to get promoted into a role that makes decisions. It is a fair question, and it misses a more practical one: how can you take on one decision, in stages, from where you are now? Most decision-shaping roles are earned by demonstrating judgement on a specific decision first.

In my judgement, a structured path over about six months is realistic for one decision, and it builds the evidence that leads to broader responsibility. The underlying shift is described in [whether AI will split analytics jobs in two](/2026-05-17-analytics-jobs-will-split-in-two-ai-will-automate-tool-operators-decision-shapers-will-control-outcomes/).

## What does AI change about shaping decisions, and what does it not change?

AI changes the volume of decisions that run through systems. Approvals, routing, prioritisation and flagging increasingly happen automatically, at scale, based on rules and models.

AI does not decide what those systems should aim for, where their limits should be or when they should hand over to people. Every automated decision has rules around it that someone should own. Those rules are where a decision shaper works.

## Find the value: which decision will you take on, and what do its errors cost?

The first two months focus on understanding one decision deeply.

**Month 1: map the decision.** Choose one decision your systems or team make repeatedly and at meaningful cost, such as approving, routing or escalating cases. Document its inputs, its rules or thresholds, who owns it and what happens afterwards. Pull a sample of recent cases and note where the outcome was right, wrong or uncertain.

**Month 2: price the errors.** Estimate what each kind of mistake costs. A wrong approval and a wrong decline usually have very different costs, in money, time and customer goodwill. Putting rough figures on both turns the decision's settings into a business question.

An illustrative example, not a documented case: an analyst working with a refund-approval process maps a sample of cases and finds that wrongly declined refunds cost far more in complaints and lost customers than the process assumed. That single finding gives them something valuable to bring to the process owner.

**Decision for you:** which recurring decision will you take on, and what does each kind of error currently cost?

## Lead the change: how do you take responsibility for the decision in stages?

The middle months turn understanding into responsibility.

**Month 3: propose limits and thresholds.** Using the error costs, propose where thresholds should sit and what the decision may do on its own. Bring the proposal to the current owner.

**Month 4: define escalation.** Write down when a case should go to a person, to whom and how quickly. Agree it with the people who would receive escalations.

**Month 5: make it adjustable and recorded.** Ensure thresholds can be changed quickly and safely, and that every change is logged with who made it, why and on what evidence.

**Month 6: hand routine cases to the system, keep the judgement.** Let routine cases run automatically within the agreed limits, while you own the exceptions, the thresholds and their review.

At each stage, the current owner of the decision should be involved, not bypassed. Earning their trust is how responsibility moves. The authority side of this is covered in [who should be allowed to act on an AI signal](/2026-08-20-assign-decision-rights-now-how-to-turn-ai-signals-into-fast-authorized-actions/).

**Decision for you:** which stage will you reach first, and whose agreement do you need for the next one?

## Prove the impact: how do you show the decision is better for your involvement?

From month five onwards, your record of changes and results is the evidence. For each change, note what you changed, why, what you expected and what happened to both kinds of error and their costs.

Review that record with the decision's owner and your manager. A decision that runs better, with fewer costly errors and clearer escalation, is the strongest argument for giving you more decisions of the same kind. **Decision for you:** how will you show, at the end of six months, that the decision runs better than it did?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The path takes sustained effort alongside your existing work, and it involves risk: proposals can be rejected, and thresholds you influence can prove wrong. It also depends on the willingness of the decision's current owner to share responsibility.

The simpler first step is month one on its own: map one decision and its recent errors. That needs no permission, takes a few days, and often produces a finding valuable enough to open the conversation about the rest. ## Questions to ask yourself

1. Which recurring decision in my area matters most, and who owns it?
2. Do I know what each kind of error in that decision costs?
3. Could I propose better limits or thresholds, with evidence?
4. Is it clear when that decision should escalate, and to whom?
5. Am I keeping a record that would show my judgement improved the decision?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a decision shaper?

A decision shaper is someone who influences or owns how a recurring decision is made: which outcome it aims for, where its thresholds sit, what errors it accepts and when it escalates to others. They use tools and analysis as inputs, but their contribution is the judgement about the decision itself.

### Do I need permission to start shaping decisions?

To own a decision fully, usually yes. But the early stages, such as mapping how a decision is made and estimating the cost of its errors, need no permission and are valuable in themselves. Bringing that analysis to whoever owns the decision is usually the best way to be given more responsibility for it.

### What does it mean to price the errors of a decision?

It means estimating what each kind of mistake costs. For example, a fraud decision can wrongly decline a genuine customer, losing a sale and some goodwill, or wrongly approve a fraudulent one, losing the value of the order. Putting rough figures on both makes the right threshold a business question rather than a technical one.

### How long does it take to move into a decision-shaping role?

It varies, but a structured approach over about six months is realistic for one decision. The time goes mostly into understanding the decision deeply, building trust with the people who own it today and showing, through a record of results, that your judgement improves outcomes.
