## Why do metrics accumulate faster than they are removed?

Most organisations find it easy to add a measure and hard to remove one. Each new question, incident or initiative brings a report, and nobody is responsible for retiring the old ones. Over the years, the few measures that drive decisions are surrounded by many that nobody uses, and the attention of leaders and analysts is spread across all of them.

Leaders who notice the clutter usually ask for a cleaner dashboard. That helps the eye, and it misses the larger question: which measures should exist at all, and how can the rest be retired without anyone losing something they genuinely need? Removing measures is not the risk most people fear, provided it is done openly and reversibly.

The principle is simple: a measure that changes no decision is not worth keeping. The practice requires care, because a few measures exist for reasons that have nothing to do with day-to-day decisions.

## What does AI change about metric clutter, and what does it not change?

AI changes how easily new measures can be produced. Reports, summaries and dashboards can now be generated in minutes, which makes clutter grow faster than before.

AI does not change the limited attention of the people reading them. More measures compete for the same hours, and the important signals become easier to miss. Deciding what not to measure remains a leadership responsibility.

## Find the value: which metrics change no decision?

Start with an inventory of every measure in regular reports and dashboards. For each, ask three questions: what decision does it support, at what threshold does someone act, and who is that person?

Sort the measures into three groups:

| Group | Test | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Keep | A named decision, threshold and owner | Remains, and is judged by the decisions it produces |
| Protect | Required by law, regulation or audit | Remains, whatever its day-to-day use |
| Pause | No decision, threshold or owner can be named | Removed from regular reports for an agreed period |

In my judgement, the pause group is usually the largest, and pausing it frees more attention than any redesign of the remaining dashboards.

**Decision for the sponsor:** how many of our reported measures can be linked to a decision, and which are protected by regulation or audit?

## Lead the change: how do leaders retire metrics without losing trust?

Retiring measures fails when it feels like something is being taken away without warning. Four practices prevent that:

- **Announce it openly.** Tell everyone who receives the reports what is being paused, why, and for how long.
- **Pause rather than delete.** Remove the measure from regular reports but keep the underlying data, so nothing is lost and the measure can return.
- **Offer a simple way back.** Anyone who needs a paused measure can ask for it, by stating the decision it supports, the threshold and who acts. If they can, it returns.
- **Protect what must stay.** Identify measures required by regulation or audit before anything is paused, and keep them.

Leaders should expect some discomfort. People are attached to familiar reports, and a few will feel exposed without them. The way back is what makes the change acceptable. Building the remaining measures into tools that trigger action is covered in [how to turn a weekly report into something that triggers action](/2026-07-23-how-to-convert-a-weekly-report-into-an-intelligence-product-that-triggers-action/).

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a risk team pauses most of the widgets on its fraud dashboards for a month before its busiest season. Two are requested back, each with a clear trigger and owner. The rest are not missed, and the team spends its meetings on the handful of signals that remain.

**Decision for the sponsor:** which measures will be paused, for how long, and who decides on requests to bring one back?

## Prove the impact: is control intact, and are decisions better?

Two kinds of evidence matter. The first is that control is intact: no regulatory requirement was missed, and the few reinstatement requests were handled quickly. The second is that the remaining measures produce more decisions: track how often each one triggers action and how quickly.

At the end of the pause period, review what happened. Measures nobody asked for can be retired permanently. Measures that were reinstated should now carry a clear decision, threshold and owner. The underlying test for each measure is set out in [which three questions decide whether a metric is worth keeping](/2026-11-12-if-a-metric-wont-change-your-next-move-cut-it-three-questions-that-make-numbers-intelligent/).

Watch two simple measures of the retirement itself: the share of remaining measures that triggered a documented action in the period, and the median time from a measure crossing its threshold to that action. If either worsens, the problem is the remaining measures, not the ones that were paused.

**Decision for the sponsor:** at the end of the pause, which measures should be retired permanently, and are the remaining ones producing decisions?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The costs are small in money and real in negotiation: reviewing every measure, agreeing what is protected, and handling the objections of people attached to particular reports. Analysts will also need time to answer reinstatement requests.

The simpler first step is to apply the three questions to one dashboard that a leadership team uses regularly, and pause what fails. That alone often shows how much attention the organisation has been spending on measures that change nothing.

Make one person responsible for the inventory of measures after the first review. Without an owner, new measures will be added as easily as before, and the clutter will return within a year. A short check against the three tests whenever a measure is added is usually enough.

## Questions to put to your team

1. How many measures appear in our regular reports and dashboards?
2. For how many can we name the decision, threshold and owner?
3. Which measures are required by regulation or audit?
4. What would we lose if the rest were paused for a quarter?
5. How would someone bring back a measure they genuinely need?

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do organisations accumulate too many metrics?

Because adding a measure is easy and removing one feels risky. Each new question, incident or initiative tends to add a report, and nobody is responsible for retiring old ones. Over time, the measures that matter are surrounded by many that nobody uses, and attention is spread too thinly to notice the important ones.

### Is it safe to stop reporting a metric?

Usually, if it is done carefully. Pausing a measure rather than deleting it, keeping the underlying data, and offering a simple way to bring it back makes the change reversible. Measures required by law, regulation or audit should be identified first and kept, whatever their use in day-to-day decisions.

### How do you decide which metrics to keep?

Keep a measure if someone can name the decision it supports, the threshold at which they act and what they do. Keep it also if regulation or audit requires it. Measures that pass neither test are candidates for pausing. The remaining set should be judged by how often it actually changes decisions.
