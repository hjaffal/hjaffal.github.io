## Why is technical mastery no longer enough to define seniority?

In many operations and analytics teams, seniority has meant technical mastery: building the best models, writing the most complex queries, knowing every corner of the tools. AI is taking over a growing share of that work. That is uncomfortable for some senior people, because it exposes whether their seniority rested on expert use of tools or on the judgements the operation depends on.

Leaders usually ask how to upskill senior staff in the new tools. That matters, and it misses the larger question: what should seniority mean when AI does much of the technical work? The answer shapes who is promoted, who is trusted with decisions and how teams are designed.

The broader research describes this kind of shift. The [International Labour Organization's 2025 study](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure) concludes that most jobs are more likely to be transformed than made redundant, because of the continued need for human input. Senior roles are a clear case: the human input they provide is changing.

## What does AI change for senior operators, and what does it not change?

AI changes the value of technical execution. Routine and even complex data work, model building and analysis are increasingly done or assisted by tools, so mastering them distinguishes people less than it did.

AI does not set the thresholds that decide what it does, choose between competing costs or decide when a case is too important for automation. Those judgements determine whether an AI-supported operation works, and they need people with experience and authority.

## Find the value: which judgements does the operation depend on?

Consider an AI-supported fraud system as an illustrative example. The model scores each transaction for risk. Below one threshold, transactions are approved automatically; above another, they are blocked or held; in between, they go to human review. The model's accuracy matters, but the thresholds decide the balance between two costs: genuine customers declined or delayed, and fraud allowed through.

Senior operators in the old sense might focus on tuning the model. Senior operators in the new sense own the thresholds and the trade-off they represent, decide what goes to review and how much review capacity is needed, and define when a new pattern should be escalated to leaders. In my judgement, those responsibilities are where seniority now lies.

**Decision for the sponsor:** which judgements does our AI-supported operation depend on, and who currently owns each one?

## Lead the change: how should leaders redefine senior roles?

Four changes align senior roles with the new work:

- **Give senior people the thresholds.** Make named senior operators responsible for the thresholds and trade-offs in each important system, with authority to change them within limits.
- **Make escalation their design.** Senior operators decide when cases go to people, which situations require leaders and how quickly.
- **Change promotion criteria.** Reward sound decisions, owned trade-offs and well-designed escalation, not only technical mastery.
- **Keep technical depth as a foundation.** Senior people still need enough understanding to question the system, but it is the starting point, not the distinction.

Leaders should expect resistance. Some senior people built their careers on technical mastery and may feel their value is being questioned. Clear expectations, and support in taking on judgement responsibilities, make the transition fairer. The wider question of who may act on what the system finds is covered in [who should be allowed to act on an AI signal](/2026-08-20-assign-decision-rights-now-how-to-turn-ai-signals-into-fast-authorized-actions/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** how will senior roles and promotion criteria change to reflect responsibility for judgement?

## Prove the impact: are senior people now improving the operation's decisions?

Measure the results of the judgements senior people own: the balance of errors at each threshold, losses and customer friction over time, the speed and quality of escalations, and how often thresholds were changed in response to evidence.

Review these with senior operators regularly. The conversation shifts from how sophisticated the technical work is to how well the operation is balancing its costs, which is the conversation that matters to the business. Related changes across analytics roles are discussed in [whether AI will split analytics jobs in two](/2026-05-17-analytics-jobs-will-split-in-two-ai-will-automate-tool-operators-decision-shapers-will-control-outcomes/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, are senior operators' judgements improving the operation's results, and where do they need support?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

Redefining seniority costs some disruption: changing role descriptions, promotion criteria and perhaps reporting lines, and supporting people through a change in what is valued. There is also a risk in giving senior operators real authority over thresholds, which must be bounded and reviewed.

The simpler first step is to choose one AI-supported system and name a senior owner for its thresholds and escalation rules, with a quarterly review of the results. That makes the new definition of seniority concrete before it is applied more widely.

It is also worth asking senior operators directly which judgements they already make informally. Often the knowledge is there, but the responsibility has never been named. Naming it is the first step to measuring it.

Finally, be explicit about how senior people develop. If the path to seniority now runs through judgement, junior staff need opportunities to make smaller decisions early, with review and support. Otherwise the organisation will have plenty of skilled tool users and too few people ready to own the thresholds when senior operators move on.

## Questions to put to your team

1. Which thresholds and trade-offs does each of our AI systems depend on?
2. Who owns each one today, formally or informally?
3. Do our promotion criteria reward judgement or technical mastery?
4. Who decides when a case is too important for automation?
5. How would we know whether our senior people's judgements are improving results?

## Frequently asked questions

### What makes someone senior in an AI-supported operation?

Responsibility for the judgements the system depends on: setting the thresholds at which it acts, owning the trade-off between competing costs, deciding when cases go to people and changing the design when the evidence shows it is wrong. Technical skill still matters, but it is no longer what distinguishes senior people.

### What is a threshold in an AI system?

A threshold is the cut-off at which an AI system takes a particular action, such as approving a transaction automatically below one risk score and holding it for review above another. Setting thresholds decides the balance between errors in each direction, which makes it a business judgement rather than a technical detail.

### Should promotion criteria change because of AI?

In many teams, yes. If promotion still rewards technical mastery of tools that AI increasingly operates, organisations will promote expert tool users into roles that now require judgement. Criteria that reward sound decisions, owned trade-offs and well-designed escalation better reflect what senior roles have become.

### Are technical skills still important for senior people?

Yes, as a foundation. Senior people need enough technical understanding to know what the system can and cannot do, to question its outputs and to design sensible thresholds. What has changed is that technical skill alone no longer justifies seniority, because more of the technical work is automated.
