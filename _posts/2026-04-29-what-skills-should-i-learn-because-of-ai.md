## Why is "learn the newest tool" incomplete advice?

Whenever AI makes headlines, the same advice appears: learn to code, take a prompt engineering course, get certified in machine learning. It is not wrong. It is incomplete. The tools change every year, many of them are themselves assisted by AI, and once everyone has learned them they stop being an advantage.

The usual question is which tools to learn. It is a reasonable starting point, and it misses the larger question: which skills keep their value as tools change? Those are the skills that sit between information and action, where someone has to decide what the information means and what to do about it.

Employers say the same. In the [World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/), analytical thinking remains the top core skill for employers, with seven out of ten companies considering it essential, while AI and big data are the fastest-growing skills.

## What does AI change about skills, and what does it not change?

AI changes the value of skills that produce standard outputs: routine writing, basic analysis, standard code and formatting. These are increasingly done by tools, and the skill becomes checking rather than producing.

AI does not change the need to frame the right problem, weigh options with incomplete information, communicate uncertainty honestly and take responsibility for a choice. If anything, it increases that need, because AI produces more options and more information for someone to judge.

## Find the value: which skills keep their value?

Four skills sit at the centre of judgement:

- **Problem framing.** Turning a vague request into a clear question tied to a decision. If the decision cannot be named, the work is not yet ready to start.
- **Trade-off ownership.** Recognising what each option gives up, recommending one and accepting the cost openly.
- **Communicating under uncertainty.** Saying how confident you are and why, rather than presenting a guess as a fact or hiding behind caveats.
- **Error detection.** Checking AI output against what you know: missing context, assumptions that do not hold and figures that look plausible but are wrong.

In my judgement, these four are worth more than any single tool, because they make every tool more useful. They also depend on knowledge of a particular business or field, which is why experience combined with good use of AI is so valuable.

**Decision for you:** which of the four skills is your weakest, and where in your current work could you practise it?

## Lead the change: how do you build these skills?

These skills are built through practice, not only through courses:

- **For problem framing,** write down the decision each piece of work is meant to inform before you start it. If you cannot, ask.
- **For trade-off ownership,** include what you are giving up in every recommendation: "I recommend this, which means we accept that."
- **For communicating uncertainty,** state your confidence in plain terms, such as "fairly confident" or "this is a guess", and explain what would change your view.
- **For error detection,** treat every AI output as a draft from a capable but uninformed colleague, and check it against what you know.

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a junior analyst asks an AI tool to explain a drop in sales. The tool produces a fluent explanation pointing to a pricing change. The analyst checks the dates and finds the drop began a week before the price changed, which leads to the real cause, a supply problem. The skill was not using the tool; it was doubting it usefully.

**Decision for you:** which of these practices will you build into your work every week from now on?

## Prove the impact: how do you know the skills are improving?

Keep a short decision journal. For each recommendation you make, note the decision, what you expected and how confident you were. Later, record what happened. Over months, the journal shows whether your judgement is improving and where it is weakest.

Share examples with your manager. Judgement is harder to see than output, and a record of decisions and their outcomes makes it visible. For a structured view of how exposed your current tasks are, the [AI Job Risk Analyzer](/ai-job-risk-analyzer/) breaks a role down task by task.

**Decision for you:** how will you track your recommendations and their outcomes, and how often will you review them?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

Building judgement is slower than taking a course, and it involves being wrong in visible ways. Some workplaces reward confident certainty over honest uncertainty, which makes the third skill uncomfortable to practise.

The simpler first step is the framing habit: for one week, write down the decision behind every piece of work before you start. It costs a few minutes a day and changes the quality of what follows. The wider positioning that these skills support is set out in [how to future-proof your career in the age of AI](/2026-04-26-how-to-future-proof-your-career-in-the-age-of-ai/).

Find someone whose judgement you respect and ask them how they reach decisions. Most experienced people are glad to explain their reasoning, and hearing it applied to real cases teaches more than any framework. Over time, you will notice which of their habits work for you and which do not.

## Questions to ask yourself

1. Do I know which decision each piece of my work is meant to inform?
2. Do my recommendations state what we would be giving up?
3. Do I say how confident I am, honestly?
4. When AI gives me an answer, how often do I check it against what I know?
5. Which of the four skills would most change how others see my work?

## Frequently asked questions

### What skills do employers value most as AI spreads?

The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 found that analytical thinking remains the top core skill for employers, with seven out of ten companies considering it essential. AI and big data are the fastest-growing skills. Employers want both: the ability to use the tools and the judgement to think with what they produce.

### Should I learn to code or take a prompt engineering course?

They can be useful, depending on your role, but they are not a long-term protection on their own. Coding and prompting are increasingly assisted by AI itself. Treat technical skills as part of a basic toolkit, and invest at least as much in judgement: framing problems, weighing options and owning decisions.

### How do you build judgement?

Mainly through practice on real decisions, followed by honest review. Make recommendations, state your confidence, record what you expected and compare it with what happened. Working alongside people with good judgement, and asking how they reached their conclusions, accelerates the process considerably.

### What does it mean to spot errors in AI output?

It means checking AI-generated work against what you know about the situation: missing context, assumptions that do not hold, figures that look plausible but are wrong, and confident statements without support. The skill depends on domain knowledge, which is why experienced people who use AI critically are so valuable.
