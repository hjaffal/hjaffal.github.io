## Why is "having the answer" no longer enough?

For a long time, expertise meant having answers that others did not. The expert was the person in the room who knew the most: the facts, the precedents, the methods. That scarcity of knowledge shaped professional identity, confidence and pay.

AI has changed the scarcity. An informed answer to many professional questions is now available to anyone in seconds, often well written and broadly correct. The natural question is whether experts are still needed. It is a fair question, and it misses the larger one: if answers are abundant, what becomes scarce? In my judgement, the scarce thing is meaning: knowing whether an answer fits this situation, what it leaves out and what should be done about it.

This shift is one of the central ideas in my book, [The Second Mind](/books/), which explores how AI is changing the way we think, create and lead. The age of the expert is not ending. It is evolving.

## What does AI change about expertise, and what does it not change?

AI changes the availability of knowledge. Facts, methods, summaries and first opinions that once required years of study to produce can be generated instantly. Being the person who knows the most is a weaker position than it was.

AI does not change the need to apply knowledge to a particular situation. It does not know the history of this team, the constraints of this budget, the people who will be affected or the risk this organisation can bear. Nor does it take responsibility. Those are where expertise now lives.

## Find the value: where does your expertise add meaning rather than answers?

Look at the moments when people come to you for help. How often do they need an answer that a well-used AI tool could give them? And how often do they need something else: help deciding which answer applies, what it means for them, or what to do when the answers conflict?

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a finance leader asks an AI tool how other companies account for a new type of contract, and receives a clear summary of the options. The value of the company's expert is not in repeating that summary. It is in knowing which option fits the company's circumstances, what the auditors will ask, and how the choice will affect next year's results.

**Decision for you:** in your field, which questions can AI now answer, and which still need your judgement to answer well?

## Lead the change: how do you build expertise around meaning?

Four habits shift expertise from answers towards meaning:

- **Use AI as a first opinion.** Let tools provide the initial answer, and spend your effort on testing it against the situation.
- **Name what is missing.** Ask what context, constraints or people the answer ignores, and say so explicitly.
- **Explain the implications.** Translate the answer into what it means for the people who will act on it.
- **Take responsibility for the decision.** Expertise that ends in a recommendation, owned by the expert, is far more valuable than expertise that ends in information.

For leaders, the implication is to value experts differently: less for what they know, more for how well they help others decide. The wider question of which skills hold their value is discussed in [whether there really are AI-proof skills](/2026-05-20-ai-proof-skills-are-a-myth/).

**Decision for you:** which of these habits will you practise first, and in which part of your work?

## Prove the impact: how do you show the value of your judgement?

Notice which of your contributions changed decisions rather than informed them. Keep a short record: the question, what the available answers said, what you added and what was decided. Over time, that record shows the part of your expertise that AI does not supply.

Share examples of it. Judgement is less visible than knowledge, and making it visible is part of being valued for it. The broader positioning is set out in [how to future-proof your career in the age of AI](/2026-04-26-how-to-future-proof-your-career-in-the-age-of-ai/).

**Decision for you:** how will you make the judgement in your expertise visible to the people who rely on it?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The shift asks experts to let go of part of their identity. Being the person with the answers is comfortable; being the person who judges answers is more exposed, because judgements can be wrong in ways that facts are not. Some organisations still reward recall more than judgement.

The simpler first step is to ask an AI tool a question you are often asked, and write down what you would add to its answer. That list is a description of your real expertise, and a guide to where to invest next.

It is also worth remembering that judgement grows by being used. Experts who hand routine answers to tools and spend the time on harder, less certain questions tend to develop faster than those who protect their role by holding on to the answers. The tools can make that growth quicker, if the time they save is used well.

## Questions to ask yourself

1. Which questions people bring me could AI now answer well?
2. What do I add that the answer alone does not provide?
3. Do I name what an answer misses, or only confirm what it says?
4. Do I end my advice with a recommendation I am willing to own?
5. Is my expertise valued for what I know or for how I help people decide?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does AI make experts less valuable?

It makes some kinds of expert knowledge less scarce, because informed answers are available to anyone in seconds. It makes other parts of expertise more valuable: judging whether an answer fits a particular situation, noticing what it misses, explaining its meaning to the people affected and taking responsibility for the decision that follows.

### What is the difference between knowledge and judgement?

Knowledge is knowing facts, methods and answers. Judgement is knowing which of them applies here, what is missing, what matters most to the people involved and what to do when the answer is uncertain. AI increasingly supplies knowledge; judgement still depends on context, experience and accountability.

### How should experts work alongside AI?

By treating AI output as a capable first opinion rather than a final answer: checking it against their knowledge of the situation, adding context it lacks, explaining its implications and making the decision. Experts who do this well can cover more ground than before while keeping the quality of their judgement.

### Is the age of the expert ending?

No, but it is changing. The expert as the person with the most information in the room is giving way to the expert as the person who can make sense of abundant information for a specific situation. That role is harder to replace, because it depends on context and responsibility rather than recall.
