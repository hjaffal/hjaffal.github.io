## Why does most loss reporting arrive too late?

Most loss reporting is lagging. Monthly losses, quarterly shrink, the number of lost shipments: each confirms what has already happened, after the moment to prevent it has passed. Lagging indicators are essential for judging results, and on their own they leave an organisation steering by what it can see behind it.

Leaders who want earlier warning usually ask for more frequent reports. Weekly instead of monthly helps a little, and it misses the larger question: which measures change before the loss does, reliably enough to act on? A faster lagging indicator is still lagging.

The prize is large. US retail shrink averaged 1.6% of sales in 2022, according to the [National Retail Federation's National Retail Security Survey 2023](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/shrink-accounted-over-112-billion-industry-losses-2022-according-nrf). Even a modest share of that, prevented through earlier warning, is worth the effort of finding indicators that genuinely lead.

## What does AI change in leading indicators, and what does it not change?

AI changes how many candidate indicators can be tested and monitored. It can search large amounts of operational data for patterns that tend to come before losses, and watch them continuously across sites, products and channels.

AI does not make an indicator trustworthy or useful. A pattern found in historical data may not hold in future, and an accurate warning is worth nothing if it reaches nobody with the authority to respond. Testing and ownership remain leadership work.

## Find the value: which indicators lead the losses you care about?

Good candidates come from understanding how losses actually happen. The following are illustrative examples, not documented cases, of leading indicators paired with the lagging result each might predict:

| Area | Possible leading indicator | Lagging result it should predict |
|---|---|---|
| Logistics | A rise in inventory not scanned at handoff points | Lost shipments and stock discrepancies |
| Fraud | Refund claims where no goods are returned | Fraud losses confirmed later |
| Service | Falling on-time delivery for a region or carrier | Complaints, refunds and customer loss |
| Stores | Repeated manual overrides at the same tills | Shrink at the next stock count |

Each candidate must then be tested. Look back over past periods: did movements in the indicator come before movements in the loss, by how long and how consistently? How often did it move without any loss following? In my judgement, many intuitive indicators fail this test, and the ones that pass are worth far more than a longer list of untested ones.

Some candidates can be ruled out before any testing. An indicator that moves only after the loss is recorded, such as an investigation opened after a stock count, is lagging however it is labelled. An indicator that the people being watched can easily change, such as a self-reported checklist, may lead nothing at all.

**Decision for the sponsor:** which leading indicators have been shown to predict our most important losses, and how much warning do they give?

## Lead the change: who acts when a leading indicator moves?

A leading indicator is only valuable if its warning reaches someone who can act in the time it provides. For each tested indicator, agree three things: the threshold that counts as a warning, the role that responds, and the response itself, such as an inspection, a process check or a tighter control.

The warning period matters. An indicator that gives two days' notice needs an owner who can act within two days; one that gives two hours needs someone on duty. Matching the owner to the warning period is where many indicator programmes fail. The authority side of this is set out in [who should be allowed to act on an AI signal](/2026-08-20-assign-decision-rights-now-how-to-turn-ai-signals-into-fast-authorized-actions/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** for each leading indicator, who responds to its warning, and can they act within the time it gives?

## Prove the impact: are the warnings preventing loss?

Report each leading indicator alongside the lagging result it is meant to predict. That pairing shows, cycle by cycle, whether warnings were followed by action and whether the action reduced the loss.

Re-test indicators regularly. Patterns change, especially where people are deliberately trying to avoid detection, and an indicator that once predicted loss well may stop doing so. Retire indicators that no longer lead, rather than letting them generate warnings nobody trusts. The board-level view of these measures is covered in [which loss prevention metrics belong in a board report](/2024-11-16-lthe-data-metrics-every-security-and-loss-prevention-team-should-track/).

Warnings should also be judged by their cost. Each false warning takes someone's time to check, and too many teach people to ignore the indicator. Record how many warnings were checked, how many led to action and how many proved unfounded, so the threshold can be tuned rather than abandoned.

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, which leading indicators still earn their place, and which should be retired?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The main costs are analysis to test candidates, data to monitor them at the right frequency, and the operational capacity to respond to warnings. False warnings cost something too: each one takes time to check.

The simpler alternative is to start with one important loss and one candidate indicator. Test it against past data, agree an owner and a response, and report the pair together for a few months. That shows whether the approach works before it is extended.

Ownership of the indicator programme itself also matters. Someone should be responsible for testing new candidates, re-testing existing ones and retiring those that no longer lead. Without that role, indicators accumulate in the same way as any other measure, and the tested ones are lost among the untested.

## Questions to put to your team

1. Which of our reported measures are leading, and which only confirm what happened?
2. Have our leading indicators been tested against the losses they claim to predict?
3. How much warning does each one give, and who acts within that time?
4. Do we report each leading indicator alongside the result it predicts?
5. Which indicators have stopped leading and should be retired?

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a leading and a lagging indicator?

A lagging indicator measures an outcome after it has happened, such as last quarter's losses or customer churn. A leading indicator measures something that tends to change before the outcome does, such as missing scans before stock losses. Lagging indicators confirm results; leading indicators give warning, if they are reliable.

### How do you test whether a leading indicator really predicts loss?

Look back over past periods and check whether movements in the indicator came before movements in the loss, by how long and how consistently. Check also how often the indicator moved without any loss following. An indicator that warns often but rarely correctly will be ignored, however sensible it sounds.

### Should leading indicators replace lagging indicators?

No. They do different jobs. Leading indicators give time to act; lagging indicators confirm whether the action worked and keep the leading ones honest. Reporting each leading indicator alongside the lagging result it predicts is the simplest way to see whether the warning is still worth trusting.
