Cancel one recurring status update this week and replace it with a decision meeting. Keep the same time block. Change the invite to spell out one decision, the owner with authority, the options on the table, the trigger thresholds, and the timebox to decide. If the decision does not happen by the end of the meeting, state what auto-executes.

Prediction: within 12 months, most companies will split calendars into two types—decisions and async reviews—and measure decision latency the way engineering teams track deploy time. Why: AI will multiply signal volume, compress analysis, and make waiting the most obvious waste in your operation. Boards will ask for time-to-decision. Legal and risk will ask who had authority. Vendors will be forced to show where their outputs bind to actions.

Here’s the difference that matters. A status update transfers information and ends with “thoughts?” A decision meeting converts a defined signal into an authorized action and ends with “we picked option B; it executes at 5 p.m.; here’s who is accountable.” If your meeting can end without a clear verb—approve, stop, raise, ship, refund, freeze—it was a status update wearing a suit.

Make this shift explicit on the calendar. Rename the invite: Decision: [Action] on [Signal]. Put the decision owner in the title. Attach a one-page brief that lists options, trigger thresholds, risks, and the default if no decision is made. Require a pre-read 24 hours before; ban slides.

Turn signals into authority before you turn them into alerts. For each AI output category, define: the owner, the action menu, the threshold that moves you from watch to act, the budget cap, and the escalation path. Publish this in a simple authority register that anyone can reference under pressure.

A real example: A national retailer’s loss prevention team rolled out an LLM that detected refund abuse and suspicious returns across stores. They went from a handful of weekly cases to hundreds of daily flags. Their weekly meeting became a readout: charts, anecdotes, and no consequences. Shrink didn’t budge.

They changed three things in two weeks. First, they created a “Refund Actions” authority map: regional LP managers could suspend self-checkout at a store for 72 hours, cap returns to ID-verified only, and trigger an immediate CCTV review—each tied to clear thresholds. Second, they converted the weekly into a daily 20-minute decision call with three people: the LP director (authority), regional lead (context), and finance partner (budget). Third, they set a default: if a store crossed the high-risk threshold and no decision was made by 3 p.m., the system auto-enabled ID-verified returns until reviewed.

Within a quarter, suspicious-return actions happened the same day, not next week. The AI vendor didn’t change a line of code; the team changed who could say “do it.” The noise didn’t go away—the indecision did.

Here’s the uncomfortable trade-off you must face: faster decisions mean accepting a higher rate of reversible mistakes, or you can keep perfect accuracy and leak value every day you wait. Pick one and declare it. If you choose speed, define your reversible actions and time-bounds upfront. If you choose thoroughness, set a cost-of-delay target and prove it’s worth the wait.

Run a decision conversion on one meeting this month:
- Pick a recurring status update with lots of data and no consequences.
- Name one decision per meeting for the next four weeks. One.
- Assign authority in writing with a budget cap. Put the person’s name in the invite.
- Pre-bind options to thresholds: “If fraud risk > X for Y days, we do Z.”
- Timebox to 20 minutes. Option A/B/C only. No new analysis unless it changes the choice.
- End each meeting with a timestamped order and owner. Publish the decision log.

Measure what matters next. Track three metrics: time from signal to decision, percent of decisions executed as ordered, and percent of decisions reversed within the time-bound window. Share them weekly, not to shame, but to tune thresholds and authority. If these numbers don’t move, you didn’t change the meeting—you changed the label.

Cut the room to the people who can change outcomes. If someone cannot authorize money, people, or policy, move them to the async pre-read. Use the meeting to decide, not to teach. Summaries and education belong in a Loom or doc sent the day before.

Bind your AI tools to actions. For each model or dashboard, add a line in the runbook: “When this alert crosses T, Owner O runs Play P within N hours.” If there is no owner, remove the alert or assign one. Do not leave it in a channel. Unowned signals are liabilities.

What strong teams do differently: they publish a living Authority Register; they define reversible vs irreversible actions; they write defaults that auto-execute on deadline; they rehearse the top three plays quarterly with the people who have the keys. Average teams increase dashboard fidelity and schedule another call.

Legal and risk partners belong in the design, not the emergency. Sit with them once and turn “blocked by legal” into clear thresholds, pre-approved language, and caps. If you need them live on every call, you haven’t done the work. Your goal is to make the safe path the easy path.

Prepare for the 12-month shift now. Tag your calendar entries as Decision or Review. Move Reviews async by default. Standardize a one-pager template for decisions (options, thresholds, risks, default). Assign a scribe to capture the order in a decision log, not meeting notes. Share the log with the people who have to act.

Final step: set a sunset date for every new alert. If it doesn’t trigger a decision within 60 days, kill it or change its owner and threshold. Signals that don’t change what happens next are expensive hobbies.

Choose a side: in the next two weeks, will you convert one status update into a true decision meeting with authority, thresholds, and a default—or will you keep attending readouts and pay for noise?
