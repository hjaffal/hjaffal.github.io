## Why does saying yes to everything make a data team less valuable?

Data teams face a constant flow of requests: a new report, another dashboard, a quick number for a meeting, a tool for a particular stakeholder. Each one seems small and reasonable, and saying yes feels helpful. Taken together, they fill the team's time with work that changes little, and leave no room for the few pieces of work that would change significant decisions.

Leaders of data teams usually ask how to deliver more. It is an understandable question, and it misses the larger one: which work deserves the team's limited time, and how can the rest be declined without damaging trust? Deciding what not to do is as important as deciding what to do.

In my judgement, the most common reason capable data teams are undervalued is not a lack of skill but a lack of focus: they are visibly busy and invisibly influential.

## What does AI change in data team priorities, and what does it not change?

AI changes the cost of many routine requests. A quick extract, a standard chart or a first-pass summary can now be produced in minutes, sometimes by the requester themselves.

AI does not change the limited time of the people who understand the business and the data well enough to shape significant decisions. If anything, it makes prioritisation more important, because the volume of possible work rises when each piece is cheaper to produce.

## Find the value: which requests support decisions that matter?

The first step is to look at where the team's time actually goes. Over a month, list the requests the team handled and, for each, the decision it supported and who made it. Many teams find a large share supported no identifiable decision at all.

Then rank incoming work against three criteria:

- **Value of the decision.** How much money, risk or customer impact depends on the decision the work informs?
- **Reach.** How many people or teams would benefit?
- **Reusability.** Would a single, reusable solution answer several requests at once?

Work that scores well on all three is the team's core. Work that scores poorly on all three should be declined, deferred or redirected to self-service.

**Decision for the sponsor:** what share of the data team's time last month supported decisions that matter, and what share supported none?

## Lead the change: how does a data team prioritise openly?

Prioritisation works when it is visible and agreed:

- **Publish the criteria.** Share how requests are ranked, so declines follow agreed rules rather than personal judgement.
- **Agree priorities with sponsors.** Review the main priorities regularly with the business leaders whose decisions the team supports.
- **Decline with an alternative.** When saying no, offer an existing report, a self-service option or a later date, and explain what the team is doing instead.
- **Favour reusable solutions.** Where several requests share a need, build one solution that serves them all.
- **Follow through on the important work.** For priority work, go beyond delivering the analysis: raise the findings with the people who decide, and follow up on what was done.

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a data team supporting operations receives a steady stream of requests for site-level dashboards. Instead of building each one, it builds a single, well-maintained view of the measures that drive operational decisions, declines the rest with an explanation, and uses the time saved to investigate the three sites where losses are rising fastest.

**Decision for the sponsor:** who agrees the data team's priorities, how often, and how will declined requests be handled?

## Prove the impact: is focusing making the team more valuable?

Measure what the team's work changes, not how much it produces. Track the decisions the team's work informed, what was decided and what happened. Track the share of time spent on priority work against the agreed criteria.

Review the results with sponsors. When they see decisions improved by the team's focused work, the case for declining low-impact requests makes itself. Freeing analysts' time from routine reporting is discussed further in [how much analyst time should go to reporting](/2026-05-26-reporting-is-not-intelligence-why-your-best-analysts-are-wasted-on-reports-and-how-to-fix-it/).

**Decision for the sponsor:** on the evidence, is the team's focused work improving decisions, and should the criteria change?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

The main cost is friction with stakeholders whose requests are declined or deferred. Some will be disappointed, and the team's leader must be willing to explain and defend the criteria. There is also a short-term risk that a declined request turns out to matter, which the regular review with sponsors is designed to catch.

The simpler first step is to record, for one month, the decision behind every request the team accepts. That record alone usually shows where focus would help, and it gives the team's leader evidence for the first conversation with sponsors. Choosing which measures deserve to exist is covered in [which three questions decide whether a metric is worth keeping](/2026-11-12-if-a-metric-wont-change-your-next-move-cut-it-three-questions-that-make-numbers-intelligent/).

It also helps to revisit the criteria themselves once or twice a year. As the business changes, the decisions that matter most change too, and a ranking that fitted last year can quietly steer the team towards yesterday's priorities. A short review with sponsors keeps the criteria honest.

## Questions to put to your team

1. Which decision did each of last month's requests support?
2. What share of our time went to work that supported no decision?
3. Do stakeholders know how we decide what to accept?
4. Which several requests could one reusable solution answer?
5. Do we follow our most important findings through to a decision?

## Frequently asked questions

### How should a data team decide which requests to accept?

By the value of the decision each request supports, the number of people who would benefit and whether a reusable solution would answer several requests at once. Requests that support no identifiable decision, or serve one person once, should be declined or deferred, with the reason explained.

### How do you say no to stakeholders without damaging relationships?

Make the criteria visible in advance, so a decline is a consequence of agreed rules rather than a personal judgement. Offer an alternative where possible, such as an existing report or a self-service option, and explain what higher-priority work the team is doing instead.

### Why do data teams struggle with prioritisation?

Because requests arrive continuously, each one seems small, and saying yes feels helpful. The cost appears only in total: a team stretched across many minor requests has no time for the few pieces of work that would change significant decisions, and its real contribution becomes hard to see.

### Who should set a data team's priorities?

The team's leader, together with the business sponsors whose decisions the team supports. Priorities set only inside the data team risk missing what the business needs; priorities set only by whoever asks loudest favour noise over value. A short regular review with sponsors balances the two.
