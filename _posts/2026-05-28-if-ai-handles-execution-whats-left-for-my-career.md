## Why is "what is left for me?" the right worry and the wrong question?

As AI takes on more execution, many people ask what is left for them. It is a reasonable worry. If tools can draft, analyse, summarise and process faster than people, the part of a career built on doing those things is shrinking.

The question, though, tends to focus on what is being taken away. The more useful question is what is being created: when tools carry out more of the work, who decides what they should do, where they should stop and what happens when they are wrong? Those decisions do not disappear. They multiply, because more work now passes through tools that need direction.

The overall picture is one of movement rather than simple loss. The [World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://www.weforum.org/press/2025/01/future-of-jobs-report-2025-78-million-new-job-opportunities-by-2030-but-urgent-upskilling-needed-to-prepare-workforces/) projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced between 2025 and 2030, with employers expecting 39% of core skills to change.

## What does AI change in a career, and what does it not change?

AI changes how work is done. It handles more of the execution: the drafting, the processing, the analysis and the routine decisions within clear rules.

AI does not decide why the work matters, when to act or what cost is acceptable. It does not know which customer will be badly affected by a rule that works well on average, or when a pattern is new enough to need a person. And it does not carry responsibility. Those remain human, and they become a larger part of the job as execution becomes cheaper.

## Find the value: where are the decisions around the tools you use?

Look at the tools already used in your area and ask four questions about each. What is it allowed to do on its own? Where does it stop and hand over to a person? What trade-off does it make, for example between speed and accuracy, or between convenience and risk? And who answers when it gets something wrong?

An illustrative example, not a documented case: a fraud system flags a small share of transactions as high risk. Before automation, an analyst reviewed each one. Now the system handles most of them. The valuable work is no longer the reviewing; it is deciding where the system's thresholds sit, which cases still need a person, and what to change when genuine customers start being declined.

In my judgement, wherever a tool now does the execution, there is a decision about its boundaries that someone should own. Those decisions are where careers are being built. A method for finding the exposed parts of your own work is set out in [which tasks are most exposed to AI automation](/2026-05-08-tasks-most-exposed-to-ai-automation/).

**Decision for you:** for the tools used in your area, which boundary decisions exist, and who owns them today?

## Lead the change: how do you build a career on the decisions around the tools?

The shift starts with small, practical steps:

- **Learn how the tools decide.** Understand enough about the tools you work with to know what they are good at, where they fail and what their limits are.
- **Find the last human decision point.** In each automated process, identify where a person can still intervene, change a setting or stop the process, and learn how that decision is made.
- **Offer to own a boundary.** Volunteer to own one threshold, rule or escalation decision, including its results.
- **State trade-offs openly.** When you recommend a change, say what it costs as well as what it gains.

**Decision for you:** which boundary decision will you offer to own, and what will you need to learn first?

## Prove the impact: how do you show the value of your judgement?

Record the boundary decisions you own: what you set, why, what trade-off it represented and what happened. When results change, note what you adjusted and why. That record shows the value of your judgement in a way that execution never did.

Share it with your manager, and use it to argue for more responsibility of the same kind. For a structured view of your current exposure, the [AI Job Risk Analyzer](/ai-job-risk-analyzer/) breaks a role down task by task.

**Decision for you:** how will you record your boundary decisions and their results, and who should see that record?

## What does it cost, and what is the simpler alternative?

Owning decisions around tools is more exposed than executing within them. When a threshold you set causes problems, it is visibly your responsibility. It also requires learning about the tools and the business in more depth than execution did.

The simpler first step is the exercise above: choose one automated process, find its last human decision point and learn how that decision is made. It costs an afternoon and shows where your next responsibility could be. The wider positioning is set out in [how to future-proof your career in the age of AI](/2026-04-26-how-to-future-proof-your-career-in-the-age-of-ai/), and the move from operating tools to shaping decisions in [how to move from operating tools to shaping decisions](/2026-06-22-how-to-become-a-decision-shaper-in-6-months-a-field-framework-for-ai-driven-ops/).

It is worth remembering that these decisions exist in almost every area, not only in technical teams. Customer service, finance, operations and marketing all now use tools that act within limits someone set. Finding out who set them, and whether they still fit, is often the most useful question nobody in the team has asked recently.

## Questions to ask yourself

1. Which tools in my area now do work that people used to do?
2. What are those tools allowed to decide on their own?
3. Where is the last point at which a person can intervene?
4. Who answers when those tools get something wrong?
5. Which of those decisions could I own?

## Frequently asked questions

### What work remains when AI handles execution?

Deciding what the tools should do, setting the limits within which they operate, choosing between competing outcomes such as speed and accuracy, deciding which cases need human attention, and taking responsibility for results. These tasks depend on context and accountability, which AI does not provide.

### What does it mean to set boundaries for AI tools?

It means defining what an AI tool may do on its own, such as approving routine cases below a certain value, and where it must stop and hand over to a person. Setting those boundaries well requires understanding both the tool and the business, and it is one of the most important judgements in AI-supported work.

### Will there be fewer jobs as AI handles execution?

Some roles built mainly on execution will shrink. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 projects 170 million jobs created and 92 million displaced between 2025 and 2030, a net increase, but with considerable movement between roles. The direction favours work built on judgement and decisions.

### How can I start moving towards judgement work?

Find one automated process in your area and identify the last point where a person can intervene. Learn how that decision is made, what it costs when it is wrong and who is responsible. Then offer to own or improve it. That single step builds the experience that judgement-based roles require.
