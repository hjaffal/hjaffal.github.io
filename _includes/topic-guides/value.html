How do you judge whether AI delivered business value?

AI delivers business value when the change produces a worthwhile customer or business outcome after its full costs and consequences are considered. Evaluating that value means asking what improved, how much of the improvement can reasonably be attributed to AI, and whether the result justifies continuing or expanding the investment.

Model accuracy, the number of generated outputs, and tool usage can help explain a system. They do not, by themselves, show that customers stayed longer, losses fell, service improved, or capacity increased. The evaluation needs to connect the capability to the work and then to the intended outcome.

Agree on the outcome and baseline before the change

Describe what success would look like in the business. Record the starting point, the measurement period, and the factors that could affect the result. Include measures that reveal an unwanted trade-off: faster service alongside resolution quality, lower losses alongside customer friction, or increased throughput alongside workload and rework.

Leading and lagging indicators provides a starting point for choosing measures. Leading indicators can signal that a workflow is changing; lagging outcomes help show whether the intended benefit followed. Neither should be treated as a substitute for understanding what happened.

Distinguish reporting from evidence for a decision

Reporting describes what happened. Intelligence helps people decide what to do next. A dashboard becomes more useful when a measure has an owner, a decision it informs, and a clear way to act. Without that connection, teams can produce increasingly detailed reports without changing the outcome.

Three questions for a decision-useful metric examines that distinction. For evaluating AI adoption, the next step is to connect the observed actions to the result, including effects that did not appear on the model scorecard.

Separate an observed improvement from AI's contribution

A result can improve during a rollout for several reasons: a policy changed, demand shifted, a team gained experience, or another system improved. Where practical, compare with a suitable control, a staged rollout, or a clearly documented baseline. Explain the limits of that comparison. A before-and-after change alone should not be presented as proof that AI caused the difference.

For example, a service team might handle more requests after introducing AI assistance and changing its escalation rules. Evaluating both changes together may establish that the new operating approach helped, while leaving uncertainty about AI's separate contribution. That uncertainty belongs in the investment discussion. This is an illustrative evaluation problem, not a measured case result.

Decide whether to continue, change, or expand

Include operating costs, human review, integration, training, support, and consequences for customers and staff. Compare the result with the alternative and the conditions agreed before investment. Continue when the evidence supports the outcome at an acceptable cost; change or stop when it does not. The aim is a more confident investment decision, including an honest account of what remains unknown.
