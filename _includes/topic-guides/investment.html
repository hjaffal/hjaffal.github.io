What makes an AI opportunity worth investing in?

An AI investment decision compares an intended business improvement with the alternatives, full cost, uncertainty, and changes required to deliver it. A capable model or an impressive demonstration is evidence about a technical possibility. It does not establish whether customers benefit, whether people can use it reliably, or whether the improvement justifies the investment.

Before committing resources, agree on the outcome, how it will be measured, and what evidence would support continuing, changing course, or stopping. The decision should remain open to a simpler tool, a process change, a smaller use case, or doing nothing.

Compare alternatives against the same outcome

Describe the current process and the constraint the proposal would remove. Then compare plausible approaches against that same problem. For a review queue, the alternatives might include clearer rules, better information for reviewers, a change in policy, AI assistance, or a combination. Compare expected quality and capacity, implementation effort, ongoing costs, and consequences when the approach is wrong.

Prioritization for data teams examines the discipline of saying no to low-impact work. That discipline is relevant to AI proposals too: scarce team capacity has an opportunity cost, even when a prototype is cheap.

Look beyond the model's price

The full cost can include preparing and maintaining data, integration, evaluation, security review, human oversight, training, process changes, support, and rework. Some costs appear outside the team paying for the software. An alert that cannot be acted on can create a queue, more coordination, or extra work for a customer-facing team.

The hidden cost of unclear ownership develops that operational argument. Before scaling, ask who can take the decision, within what limits, and what capacity they have to handle the output. Better detection has little practical value when the organization cannot respond to it.

Use a test to reduce decision uncertainty

A useful test targets the assumptions most likely to change the investment decision. Can the team use the output? Is quality sufficient for the proposed action? Does the workflow improve after review and correction time are included? Which costs increase with volume? A pilot that cannot answer those questions may produce activity without making the decision clearer.

For example, an AI-assisted review process might reduce time spent on straightforward cases but increase specialist review of exceptions. Evaluate both effects against the baseline. This is an illustrative trade-off, not a promised result.

Make the reasons to walk away explicit

Stop or reconsider when the improvement is too small, the alternative is more effective, adoption requires changes the organization cannot support, or the downside cannot be managed within acceptable limits. Agreeing those conditions before a test makes it easier to interpret an inconvenient result. For an idea that proceeds, the next question is how the work, responsibilities, and skills need to change.
